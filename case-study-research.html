What changed when the language classroom moved online?

A qualitative study of challenges experienced by university learners during the transition from classroom-based foreign-language education to online learning.

Participants
15 university-level learners
Method
Semi-structured qualitative interviews
Focus
Learner experience during transition
Status
Completed M.A. thesis

Research trail

01 / Question

Starting from a disrupted learning experience.

The study asked how learners experienced the move from face-to-face foreign-language teaching to online learning, and which challenges became most visible in that transition.

The research sits at the intersection of computer-assisted language learning, learner motivation and the design of online learning environments. It treats the transition as a lived educational change—not simply a switch of delivery platform.

02 / Method

A qualitative lens on learner experience.

15participants
1:1semi-structured interviews
Qual.structured analysis

Participant accounts were used to identify recurring challenges and differences in experience. The thesis includes a literature review, research design, evidence, analysis, recommendations and explicit limitations.

Privacy safeguard: this public case shares the research structure and high-level themes only. It does not reproduce names, contact details, raw responses or information that could identify participants.

03 / Themes

What learners’ accounts made visible.

01

Motivation & engagement

Online environments changed the conditions for attention, persistence and participation.

02

Interaction & group work

Peer communication and collaborative learning required more intentional design.

03

Format & technology

Synchronous and asynchronous modes created different affordances and constraints.

04

Assessment & feedback

Evaluation practices and teacher relationships shaped confidence and progress.

05

Learner variability

Needs, preferences, prior knowledge and access conditions influenced the transition.

06

Platform conditions

Technical and interface problems could become learning problems.

04 / Design implications

Online learning needs more than content transfer.

  • Design participation.Create visible structures for interaction, collaboration and social presence.
  • Use multiple modes deliberately.Match synchronous and asynchronous activity to the learning purpose.
  • Make feedback continuous.Build clear, timely feedback and support into the learning rhythm.
  • Plan for variability.Offer scaffolds, pathways and access options without assigning fixed learner labels.

05 / Boundaries

What this study does—and does not—claim.

The study offers qualitative insight into a defined participant group and transition context. It does not claim statistical generalizability or establish causal effects. The public summary intentionally avoids turning participant experiences into performance metrics that were not measured.
